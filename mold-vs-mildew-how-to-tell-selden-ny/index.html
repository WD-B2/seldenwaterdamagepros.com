Mold vs Mildew: How to Tell the Difference in a Selden, NY Home

The two terms overlap, so appearance and location are better guides than the label.

Mildew is usually flat, powdery or downy growth that sits on a surface, often white, gray or yellowish. Mold is the wider word, and it covers fuzzy, slimy or speckled growth in many colors that can go deeper into a material. In everyday speech the two get swapped, and mildew is technically a kind of mold.

You cannot confirm either one by eye alone, and nothing in this post is a diagnosis. What you can do is read the clues, such as color, texture and where it sits, and decide whether it is a wipe-down job or a reason to bring in help. That is the practical question for most households in Selden, NY.

What each one tends to look like

These are tendencies, not rules. Lighting, surface texture and age change how growth appears, so use the table as a starting point for asking better questions.

ClueOften called mildewOften called mold
TextureFlat, powdery or downy filmFuzzy, slimy, bumpy or speckled patches
ColorWhite, gray, pale yellowBlack, green, blue-green, brown, orange or pink
Where it sitsOn the surface of a materialOn the surface and sometimes into it
Typical settingShower tile, window sills, grout, damp fabricDrywall, ceiling edges, carpet backing, wood, insulation
SmellLight, damp or sourStronger, earthy or musty

Color alone tells you very little. Dark patches may be dirt and pale patches may run deeper than they look. If the material is porous and soft, assume the patch goes further than the stain.

Look at where it is, not just what it is

Location often answers the question faster than color does. Growth that appears only on a bathroom surface that gets wet every morning points to ordinary humidity. Growth on a wall that never sees a shower points to something else, usually a water source you cannot see.

Bathroom grout and caulk

Usually a ventilation and drying issue. Surface growth on tile responds to cleaning and a working exhaust fan.

Window sills and frames

Condensation collects here in cold weather. A wipe-down plus better airflow often settles it, though a window leak is worth checking.

Closet corners and exterior walls

Cool, still air plus furniture pushed against the wall traps moisture. Pull items out and take a look at the back of the drywall.

Ceilings and wall bases

Often a leak from above or beside. This is a moisture investigation, not just a cleaning job.

If the stain sits under a bathroom, behind a washer or along an exterior wall after rain, treat it as a water problem first. Drywall paper, ceiling tile and carpet backing are where small patches become larger jobs.

What you can do right now

  • Photograph the area with a ruler or coin beside it for scale, and note the date.
  • Look for the water: dripping pipes, a damp baseboard, sweating windows, a stained ceiling above.
  • Run a bathroom fan during and for some time after showers, and keep doors open for airflow.
  • Wipe hard surfaces like tile, glass and sealed counters with detergent and water. Wear gloves and a mask, and never mix cleaning products.
  • Leave soft materials such as drywall, ceiling tile and carpet alone until someone has looked at them.
  • Check humidity with an inexpensive meter, since a room that stays damp will grow it back.

A dehumidifier helps, but only as a follow-up to fixing the cause. If the room's humidity does not come down after a few days, or a patch returns within weeks of cleaning, something is still feeding it.

When a wipe-down is not enough

Call when growth is on porous material, covers more than a small spot, returns after cleaning, or comes with a wet wall or ceiling. At that point it is not a mildew-or-mold question anymore. It is a question of how far moisture has gone and what has to be removed.

A crew uses moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera to find the wet area, then decides whether the work is structural drying, mold remediation, or both. We explain the scope and price before work starts, and our licensed and insured crews are dispatched around the clock.

Health questions go to a physician

We do not diagnose mold, mildew or any reaction to them. If anyone in your home has symptoms they connect to the air indoors, talk with a physician. Do not rely on a website, including this one, for that.

Licensing, testing and who does what in New York

New York's State Mold Program, under Labor Law Article 32, requires state licences for mold assessors and remediators. The same individual cannot do both assessment and remediation on one property. An independent assessment is used to define the scope of work, so if you want to know what you actually have, that is the person to ask.

A hardware-store test kit or a lab result from a kit does not replace that assessment. If you hire anyone, ask to see their licence. Homeowners may also ask any company whether it works to ANSI/IICRC S520 practice for mold.

What this means for Selden homes

Selden's housing includes houses from the 1950s through 1970s, which may have older drywall, paint layers and finished basements. A pre-1980 house may have materials that need testing before demolition, so do not cut out a stained wall to find out what is behind it. Ask the right professional first.

Long Island summers bring humid air, and cool basement walls or closet corners are where it settles. Check your water bill to confirm your supplier, and if a bathroom or basement drain smells or backs up, ask whether your home is on a sewer or a private septic system, because backups call for a plumber first.

The same clues apply in Mount Sinai, where harbor-edge homes should check the FEMA flood map for their address, and in Middle Island, where homes near lakes and river headwaters can stay damp. For any rebuild, ask the Town of Brookhaven Division of Building about permits, since rules vary.

Questions we hear in Selden

Is mildew less serious than mold?

The word does not tell you that. Mildew is a form of mold, and what matters for your house is the material it is on and the moisture behind it. We make no health statements, so questions about effects belong with a physician.

Can I tell the difference by color?

Not reliably. Many colors show up on both, and color shifts with age and light. Texture, location and whether the material is soft or hard are more useful clues.

Does bleach solve it?

On a hard, sealed surface, detergent and water are usually enough. On drywall, wood or carpet, surface cleaning does not reach what is below, and the moisture must be fixed anyway.

Do I need a lab test to know which it is?

Not for a small patch on tile. For larger or recurring growth, a licensed independent assessor can set a scope, and a lab sample is only one tool they may use.

The corner of my Selden bathroom ceiling has dark spots. Where do I start?

Check whether the fan works and whether anything leaks from above, such as a tub, toilet or roof. If the ceiling is soft, stained, or the spots return, a moisture reading will show whether the cause is humidity or a leak.
