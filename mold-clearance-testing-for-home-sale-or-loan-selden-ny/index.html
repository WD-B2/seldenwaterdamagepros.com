Mold Clearance Testing for a Home Sale or Loan in Selden, NY

Clearance is a written check, done by someone other than the remediator, that mold work was completed to the planned scope.

Mold clearance, also called a post-remediation assessment, is an independent inspection after mold has been removed. A licensed assessor looks at the work area, compares it with the written scope, and records whether the job met the standard. In New York it cannot be done by the same individual who did the removal.

People in Selden, NY usually ask about it for one reason: a sale or a loan is waiting on paperwork. This post explains what clearance is, who performs it, what the steps look like, and what it does and does not tell a buyer or a lender.

What clearance actually is

Think of clearance as a final inspection of mold work, similar in spirit to a building inspection after a renovation. The assessor reviews the area, checks that materials named in the scope were handled, looks for remaining moisture, and may collect samples depending on the plan. The result is a written report.

Under the New York State Mold Program, Labor Law Article 32, a post-remediation assessment is used as a minimum work standard. It is not a health statement about the house, and it does not say mold can never return. It says the work met the plan on the day it was checked.

Who is allowed to do it

New York requires state licences for mold assessors and for mold remediators. The law bars the same individual from doing both assessment and remediation on the same property. An independent assessment also defines the scope of remediation before work starts, so the assessor is involved at the beginning and the end.

RoleWhat they doCannot also be
Mold assessor (licensed)Inspects, writes the scope, later performs the post-remediation assessmentThe remediator on that property
Mold remediator (licensed)Contains, removes and cleans according to the scopeThe assessor on that property
Restoration and drying crewDries the structure and handles water damage that caused the moldNot a substitute for either licence

When you hire anyone, ask to see the licence for the role they claim. A company that tells you it can test and remove in one package, on one property, deserves a direct question about how it complies with the independence rule.

The usual order of a job that ends in clearance

  1. The water source is repaired by the appropriate trade.
  2. A licensed assessor inspects and writes a scope of work.
  3. A licensed remediator contains the area, runs HEPA air scrubbers and removes or cleans materials per the scope, working to ANSI/IICRC S520 practice.
  4. Remaining moisture is dried and checked with moisture meters.
  5. The assessor returns for the post-remediation assessment and issues a written report.
  6. Rebuilding starts after that report, since closing walls too early can hide the area you are trying to verify.

Notice that the assessor appears twice. If you only call a remediator, you may have clean walls but no independent record. That gap tends to matter later, when someone else is asking whether the work was done properly.

Buyer and lender situations, described neutrally

Sales and loans bring other people into the picture, and each has its own requirements. We cannot speak for any buyer, agent or lender, and nothing here predicts whether a report will be accepted. What follows are common situations and the questions worth asking.

SituationWhat is commonly asked forQuestion to put to the other party
Seller finds mold before listingA scope, a remediation record and a post-remediation reportWhich documents does the listing agent want attached?
Inspection during a sale turns up growthRemediation and independent confirmation before closingWhat does the contract allow for timing and who pays?
Lender raises mold as a conditionWritten proof the work was completedWhat exact wording or form does the lender require?
Buyer wants reassurance, no growth foundPossibly an independent assessmentIs the buyer paying for it, and is the access agreed?

Requirements differ from lender to lender and contract to contract. The safest step is to ask in writing what documents they need, then give that list to the assessor before the job starts, so the report is written for the right audience.

Where drying and documentation fit

Most mold jobs start with a water event. Until wet materials meet a dry reference, a clearance visit has little to confirm, because lingering moisture would invite regrowth. Structural drying with air movers, LGR dehumidifiers and meter logs gives the assessor a moisture record to review.

Keep a folder with photos taken before work, the assessor's scope, the remediator's invoice and completion notes, drying logs, and the final report. We document damage with photos, moisture readings and a written scope for our own jobs, and that kind of record also supports a homeowner's insurance claim. For the water event behind the mold, coverage usually turns on whether it was sudden and accidental or gradual, so read your policy or ask your insurer.

Questions we hear in Selden

Is clearance the same as mold testing?

Not quite. Testing can mean air or surface sampling at any time. Clearance means the post-remediation assessment after removal, compared with the written scope. The assessor decides what sampling, if any, belongs in it.

Can the company that removed the mold also clear it?

Not on the same property. New York law bars the same individual from doing both, and the independent assessment is meant to check the work from the outside.

Does a clearance report mean a buyer or lender will accept the house?

No. Buyers and lenders set their own conditions, and a report is evidence, not a ruling. Ask them in writing what they require.

Should I clean the mold first or hire an assessor first?

Call the assessor first when you can. The assessor writes the scope, and cleaning on your own before that may remove evidence the scope needs.

I am selling a Selden home with an old basement stain. Where do I begin?

Find out whether the stain is dry and old or active. A moisture meter reading helps, and an assessor can tell you whether anything needs a scope. Talk to your agent about what the buyer's side will ask for.
